PHILIPPINE HISTORY • 1896

The Cry of the Philippine Revolution

Introduction and Popular/Conflicting Accounts of the Cry: Understanding Dr. Pio Valenzuela's Testimony and the Different Historical Interpretations.

Explore History

OVERVIEW

Introduction to the Cry

What Was the Cry?

The Cry refers to the historic events in August 1896 associated with the beginning of the Philippine Revolution against Spanish colonial rule. These events involved members of the Katipunan, a secret revolutionary organization founded in 1892 to pursue Philippine independence.

After Spanish authorities discovered the Katipunan in August 1896, its members gathered north of Manila. The event remembered as the Cry is associated with a decision to begin an uprising and the tearing of cedulas, the community tax certificates carried under Spanish rule.

Accounts disagree about the date, meeting place, sequence of events, and who witnessed them. Balintawak, Kangkong, Bahay Toro, and Pugad Lawin appear in different accounts; the dispute is about how those places and meetings relate to the Cry.

What did the Cry represent?

  • A public break with Spanish colonial authority.
  • The Katipunan's move toward armed revolt.
  • A symbol remembered through the cedula story.
  • A contested event studied through eyewitness accounts.

HISTORICAL FIGURE

Dr. Pio Valenzuela

A Revolutionary and Historical Witness

Dr. Pio Valenzuela was a Filipino physician and revolutionary who became involved in the Katipunan. He was associated with Andres Bonifacio and other revolutionary leaders during the struggle against Spanish colonial rule.

Valenzuela is an important figure in discussions about the Cry because his accounts have been used in examining the event's date, location, and circumstances.

His testimony must be studied alongside other historical accounts. As with other eyewitness recollections, historians need to consider when a statement was made, what it describes, and whether other evidence supports it.

Historical focus: Valenzuela's recollections and the differences between historical testimonies about the Cry.

PRIMARY SOURCE ANALYSIS

Valenzuela's Memoirs

SOURCE ANALYSIS - PARAPHRASE

What Valenzuela's memoir contributes

Pio Valenzuela's Memoirs of the K.K.K. and the Philippine Revolution is a first-person account. The English edition catalogued by the University of the Philippines Diliman Library was translated by Luis Serrano and published in 1946.

In Teodoro Agoncillo's 1961 reading of the memoir, Valenzuela's group moved from Balintawak through Kangkong and reached Pugad Lawin on August 23, where Agoncillo places the Cry.

This is a paraphrase of Agoncillo's interpretation, not a direct quotation or an uncontested conclusion.

Understanding His Account

Agoncillo wrote that Valenzuela's memoir was prepared years after the event from notes he said he had made in 1897. That makes the memoir valuable evidence, but historians still need to compare its different versions and publication history.

Agoncillo favored Valenzuela's account. Nicolas Zafra questioned that choice and argued that Santiago Alvarez's account also deserved consideration. Their disagreement is part of the historical debate, not proof that one account is automatically correct.

DIFFERENT PERSPECTIVES

Popular and Conflicting Accounts of the Cry

Explore the witnesses and historical interpretations commonly discussed in connection with the Cry.

01

Dr. Pio Valenzuela

In the memoir as interpreted by Agoncillo, the Cry was at Pugad Lawin on August 23, after the group moved through Balintawak and Kangkong.

Agoncillo treated Valenzuela as an eyewitness but acknowledged that his testimony changed across time. Compare the memoir with earlier statements before treating the date and place as settled.

02

Santiago Alvarez

Alvarez's memoir describes gatherings at Bahay Toro on August 23 and 24, including a large meeting led by Bonifacio. His account gives another sequence and date for the events.

Historians have disagreed about whether Alvarez witnessed the Cry or recorded information from others. Agoncillo challenged its reliability; Zafra argued it should still be considered. See their debate in the cited sources.

03

Guillermo Masangkay

Masangkay's published account places a major meeting on August 26 at Apolonio Samson's house in Balintawak. He describes Bonifacio presiding and the group deciding to rise against Spain.

His account connects the meeting, decision to revolt, and cedula tearing. It differs in date and place from the Pugad Lawin and Bahay Toro accounts.

04

Why do the names differ?

Balintawak, Kangkong, Bahay Toro, and Pugad Lawin appear in the record. The name "Balintawak" was also used for a wider area, which can make comparisons between locations difficult.

The government history overview records August 23 at Pugad Lawin and August 26 at Balintawak among the officially endorsed dates. Other testimony gives August 24 or 25. Keep these claims attributed to their sources rather than declaring one proven.

Comparison of Historical Accounts

HISTORICAL INVESTIGATION

Why Do Historical Accounts Conflict?

01

Human Memory

People may remember the same event differently, especially after many years have passed.

02

Time of Recording

An account written years after an event may differ from a statement recorded closer to the event.

03

Different Perspectives

Witnesses may have observed different parts of an event or understood its significance differently.

04

Historical Evidence

Historians compare documents, testimonies, and other evidence to evaluate competing explanations.

IMPORTANT EVENTS

Historical Timeline

1892 - 1896

Katipunan and discovery

The Katipunan was founded in 1892. On August 19, 1896, Spanish authorities learned of the society, forcing members to move and organize in the hills north of Manila.

Aug. 20 - 26

Meetings and the Cry

Witnesses describe meetings and the decision to revolt at different dates and places. Valenzuela's later memoir points to Pugad Lawin; Alvarez describes Bahay Toro; Masangkay names Balintawak.

Aug. 30, 1896

Armed revolution begins

Katipuneros launched attacks, including the Battle of San Juan del Monte. The uprising spread into an armed revolution against Spanish rule.

FINAL REFLECTION

Conclusion

The conflicting accounts of the Cry demonstrate that understanding history requires more than memorizing dates and names. Historians must examine historical documents, compare eyewitness testimonies, and evaluate the circumstances in which accounts were recorded.

Dr. Pio Valenzuela's testimony is an important part of this historical discussion. However, his account should be studied alongside the recollections of other revolutionaries and reliable historical research.

By comparing evidence carefully, students can develop a deeper understanding of the Philippine Revolution and learn why historical interpretations sometimes differ.

The safest conclusion is that the Cry marks a turning point, while its precise date, location, and sequence remain disputed. Each claim should be tied to the source that reports it.
